Healthcare software must be fast, available, easy to use, traceable and maintainable, because slow or unavailable systems delay patient care and unclear or unauditable systems create safety and compliance risks. Typical targets used by hospitals for web-based patient-facing systems are page and search responses within two to three seconds under normal load, availability of 99.5 percent or better during service hours, no loss of confirmed bookings or records after a failure, and complete audit trails for every access to patient data. These targets are agreed with the hospital before design and checked by testing before release. Security, privacy, performance, availability, usability and auditability should each be covered by at least one measurable requirement, and every requirement needs an acceptance criterion that a tester can check. The people who approve the requirements and the chosen development approach are the project manager, the software architect, the security team and the compliance officer.

Performance. Common actions such as searching for a doctor, viewing available slots, logging in and confirming a booking respond within about two to three seconds for the large majority of requests under normal load. The system must remain usable during peak periods, for example Monday mornings or after a reminder message is sent to many patients at once, and must be load tested with realistic numbers of concurrent users before go-live. Reports and exports that take longer run in the background and notify the user when ready.

Availability and reliability. The patient-facing service is available during clinic hours with an agreed uptime target, and planned maintenance is announced in advance and scheduled outside busy periods. Failures of one component, such as the reminder service, must not stop booking. Data is backed up regularly, restores are tested, and the recovery time and the maximum acceptable data loss are defined. When the system is down, staff have a documented manual fallback so that patients can still be seen.

Scalability. The system must grow from one clinic or branch to many without redesign, handle a growing number of doctors, patients and appointments, and allow capacity to be added without long downtime.

Usability and accessibility. Patients of all ages and digital skill levels should complete common tasks without training. Screens work on small phones and slow networks, support more than one language, use large readable text and sufficient contrast, and can be used with screen readers and keyboard navigation. Staff screens support fast data entry with few clicks, and errors are explained in plain language with a clear next step.

Auditability and traceability. Access to and changes of patient data, permission changes, administrator actions, consent decisions and system configuration changes are logged with user, time, action and outcome. Logs are protected from alteration, searchable and kept for the defined retention period. Each requirement can be traced to its source stakeholder or regulation, to the design element that implements it and to the test that verifies it, so that auditors can follow a requirement from origin to evidence.

Security of the application. The software is developed with secure coding practices and reviewed for common web vulnerabilities such as injection, broken access control and insecure session handling. It is tested by security testing before each major release, dependencies are kept up to date, secrets are not stored in code, and security fixes can be released quickly without waiting for the next feature release.

Data management. Data has defined owners, formats and quality rules. The system avoids duplicate patient records, validates inputs, keeps history of changes and supports export of a person's data in a common format. Retention and deletion rules are defined per data type and applied automatically where possible.

Interoperability and integration. The system connects to the hospital information system, electronic health record, laboratory, billing and notification services through documented interfaces that follow recognised healthcare standards. Interfaces are versioned, monitored and tested, and failures in an external system are handled gracefully with retries and clear messages.

Maintainability and operations. The code and configuration are documented and covered by automated tests so that changes can be made safely. Monitoring, alerting and log aggregation allow the support team to detect problems early. Releases follow a controlled process with testing, approval and rollback, and the system provides separate environments for development, testing and production.

Testing and documentation. Formal test plans, test evidence and traceability between requirements and tests are required, because the hospital must be able to show auditors and regulators that the system does what it was approved to do. User and administrator documentation, training material and a record of approved changes are delivered with each release.

Project context. In a typical hospital appointment project the core requirements are fairly stable and well understood, regulatory and privacy obligations are strong, patient data makes security risk high, the system has a moderate number of modules and user roles, it must connect to some existing hospital systems, and clinical and management stakeholders can be reached for feedback and approval at planned points. Failure of the system disrupts care and can lose data but does not by itself physically harm a patient, unlike software that controls medical devices. Features such as reminders, reports and the patient app may change more often than the compliance and data-protection core, and releases need controlled approval rather than uncontrolled continuous deployment.

Human oversight. Requirements, compliance mappings and the chosen development approach generated or suggested by automated tools are advisory until reviewed and approved by the responsible people, and the approval is recorded with the name, role and date.
